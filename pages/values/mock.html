Run a 45-minute values interview

1:30
Press “Draw” and answer aloud before opening any notes.
Tell me about a difficult conversation. What exactly did you say? Tell me about feedback that was hard to hear and what changed afterward. When did you communicate bad news or a technical risk early? Tell me about balancing delivery speed with quality or safety. Describe a time you or the team missed the mark. How did you fix it? When did you deliberately reduce scope or make a reversible decision? Tell me about a time you protected the customer when it was inconvenient. How did customer evidence change your technical plan? What customer-facing invariant would you refuse to compromise? Tell me about helping someone who was not succeeding. When did you put the team's goals above personal ambition? Describe a cross-team collaboration where you changed your own approach. Tell me about an improvement you initiated without being asked. When did you advocate for a change that faced resistance? What change you drove continued after the project ended?

Five-question mock agenda

0-3

Opening

Brief introduction. Do not spend the round retelling your resume.

3-10

Open company

Use the production disagreement or feedback story; probe exact words and accountability.

10-17

Build with heart and balance

Use pricing fallback/aggregation or staged migration; probe the rejected shortcut.

17-24

Customer

Use pricing correctness, Promo UI or Wally streaming; probe the user-visible consequence.

24-31

Team

Use cross-team triage or mentoring; probe what you gave up and how dependency was reduced.

31-38

Change

Use Spark consolidation or Promo UI; probe adoption, evidence and durability.

38-45

Reflection and your questions

One failure/learning question, then ask about how values appear in engineering decisions.

Follow-up interruption bank

That sounds technical. What did you personally do with people?

Name the risk you surfaced, the question you asked, how you adapted the message, who you aligned and how you followed through.

Why was the other person's view reasonable?

State their constraint accurately before defending your choice. This demonstrates empathy and judgment rather than victory.

What did you sacrifice?

Use a real cost: scope, time, preferred architecture, individual progress, short-term simplicity or visibility. Do not claim sacrifice when no trade-off existed.

How did you know the customer needed that?

Point to support evidence, product input, observed behavior, data reconciliation or a customer-visible correctness invariant. Do not imply direct research you did not perform.

What was your mistake?

Own one specific assumption or behavior. Avoid converting the answer into another success story.

What would this person say about you now?

Address both the result and the relationship. Intent alone is not evidence that communication was effective.

What changed permanently?

Name a durable behavior, contract, test, dashboard, process, ownership mechanism or independent capability.

Score each answer from 1 to 5

Specificity

One event, clear roles, actual choice.

Tension

Two valid goals or perspectives competed.

Behavior

Words, actions and adaptation are visible.

Evidence

Result is verified, not aspirational.

Reflection

Learning changed later behavior.

Control

Core answer ends within 90 seconds.

Questions to ask the values interviewer

  • Which value creates the most useful tension in day-to-day engineering decisions?
  • Can you share an example of a recent decision where two Atlassian values pulled in different directions?
  • How do teams make “open company” work well across distributed or cross-functional groups?
  • How are customer signals brought into platform or infrastructure engineering teams?

Closing reminder

I do not need to sound perfect. I need to be specific, accountable and consistent: a real situation, a difficult choice, behavior another person could have observed, a result I can defend, and a lesson that changed how I work.
